title: Keeping cash, signals and stock dividends
---
Instead of paying cash out, a firm can keep it. And whatever it does, investors read it as a signal.

::: rule
- **Retaining cash:** in perfect markets, holding cash (a zero-NPV investment) changes nothing. With **taxes**, the interest earned on cash is taxed at the corporate level first, so hoarding cash costs shareholders. It can also tempt managers to waste it (**agency costs**).
- **Signals:** managers **smooth** dividends and raise them only when they expect to keep them up. So a dividend increase is good news and a **cut is bad news**. A buyback can signal managers think the shares are **undervalued**.
- **Stock dividends and splits** add shares but no value; firms use them to keep the share price in a "normal" trading range.
:::

::: example
A firm keeps \$100M of cash earning 5%, with a 26% corporate tax rate. The \$5M of interest is taxed \$1.3M a year before shareholders see any of it. Paid out, the cash would have avoided that corporate tax.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=numeric answer="1.3" tolerance=0.01
A firm holds \$100M of cash earning 5% and its tax rate is 26%. How much corporate tax does it pay on the interest each year, in millions?
solution: $100 \times 0.05 \times 0.26 = 1.3$ million.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=choice
A firm unexpectedly raises its dividend. Its share price will most likely:
- [x] rise, because it signals managers expect higher earnings
- [ ] fall, because less cash is left in the firm
- [ ] not change
solution: Managers avoid raising dividends they might have to cut, so a raise signals confidence.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=choice
Dividend smoothing means firms:
- [ ] pay all their earnings as dividends each year
- [x] change dividends rarely and gradually, even when earnings jump around
- [ ] pay dividends only in good years
solution: Dividends are much steadier than earnings.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=choice
A firm announces a large share buyback. Investors may read this as a sign that managers think the shares are:
- [x] undervalued
- [ ] overvalued
- [ ] about to be split
solution: Managers buying shares on the firm's behalf suggests they think the price is too low.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=numeric answer="220" tolerance=0.01
You own 200 shares. The firm pays a 10% stock dividend. How many shares do you own now?
solution: $200 \times 1.10 = 220$ shares, but your share of the firm is unchanged.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
A firm announces a dividend cut. The price usually:
- [ ] rises, since more cash stays in the firm
- [x] falls, since investors fear earnings are in trouble
- [ ] doesn't react
solution: Because dividends are smoothed, a cut suggests managers expect lasting trouble.
:::
