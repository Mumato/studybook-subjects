---
id: eqd-02
title: Seasoned offerings and rights offers
---
Public firms raise more equity through **seasoned equity offerings** (SEOs), often by a rights offer in Canada.

::: rule
- **Cash offer:** new shares sold to any investor through underwriters.
- **Rights offer:** existing shareholders get **rights** to buy new shares at a **subscription price** below market, so they can keep their share of the firm.
- With $N$ rights needed per new share, the **theoretical ex-rights price** is
$$TERP = \frac{N \times P + S}{N + 1}$$
and each right is worth $P - TERP$.
- SEO announcements usually lower the share price (managers may be selling overvalued shares). Issue costs are high.
:::

::: example
Shares trade at \$30. One new share can be bought at \$25 for every 4 held: $TERP = (4 \times 30 + 25) / 5 = 29$. Each right is worth \$1.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=numeric answer="29" tolerance=0.01
Shares trade at \$30 and holders can buy one new share at \$25 for every 4 they own. What is the theoretical ex-rights price?
solution: $(4 \times 30 + 25) / 5 = 29$.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=numeric answer="1" tolerance=0.01
What is each right worth?
solution: $30 - 29 = 1$.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=numeric answer="19.17" tolerance=0.01
Shares trade at \$20; one new share can be bought at \$15 for every 5 held. What is the ex-rights price?
solution: $(5 \times 20 + 15) / 6 = 19.17$.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=choice
A shareholder who neither uses nor sells their rights:
- [ ] is unaffected
- [x] loses value as the share price drops
- [ ] gains, since the price rises
solution: The price falls to the ex-rights price; the rights were the compensation. Ignoring them throws that value away.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=choice
When a public firm announces a seasoned equity offering, its share price on average:
- [ ] rises
- [x] falls
- [ ] doesn't change
solution: Investors infer managers think the shares are overvalued (asymmetric information).
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
The main purpose of a rights offer is to:
- [x] let existing shareholders keep their share of the firm
- [ ] avoid paying any dividends
- [ ] sell shares above market price
solution: Each holder can buy their share of the new issue, so ownership isn't diluted.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=open creative=true
IPOs are underpriced on average, yet many investors who buy IPOs don't earn high returns. How can both be true?
solution: The "winner's curse": in attractive IPOs, demand is high and small investors get few shares; in poor IPOs they get all they asked for. Their average return is much lower than the average first-day return.
:::
