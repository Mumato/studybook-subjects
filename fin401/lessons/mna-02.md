---
id: mna-02
title: Valuing a takeover
---
An acquisition is a project: it makes sense only if what you get exceeds what you pay.

::: rule
- **Takeover premium** = (offer price − pre-bid price) ÷ pre-bid price.
- For the acquirer:
$$NPV = \text{synergies} - \text{premium paid}$$
where the **premium paid** is the price paid minus the target's standalone value.
- Highest sensible price for the target = standalone value + synergies.
- **Stock deal:** the **exchange ratio** is the number of acquirer shares given per target share $= \dfrac{\text{offer price per target share}}{\text{acquirer share price}}$.
- **EPS bootstrapping:** buying a firm with a lower P/E with shares raises EPS, but creates no value by itself.
:::

::: example
A target worth \$400M alone is bought for \$480M; synergies are worth \$120M. The acquirer's NPV is $120 - 80 = 40$ million.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=numeric answer="30" tolerance=0.01
A target trades at \$40 before the bid. The offer is \$52 a share. What is the takeover premium? Type the percent.
solution: $(52 - 40) / 40 = 30\%$.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=numeric answer="40" tolerance=0.01
A target worth \$400M on its own is bought for \$480M. The synergies are worth \$120M. What is the acquirer's NPV, in millions?
solution: Premium paid $= 80$; $NPV = 120 - 80 = 40$ million.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=numeric answer="50" tolerance=0.01
A target has 20 million shares at \$40. Synergies would be worth \$200M. What is the most the acquirer should pay per share?
solution: $(800 + 200) / 20 = 50$.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=numeric answer="0.6" tolerance=0.001
The acquirer's shares trade at \$60 and the target's at \$30. The acquirer offers a 20% premium, paid in its own shares. What is the exchange ratio?
solution: Offer $= 30 \times 1.2 = 36$; $36 / 60 = 0.6$ acquirer shares per target share.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=numeric answer="2.14" tolerance=0.01
An acquirer earns \$100M with 50M shares at \$40 (EPS \$2). It buys a target earning \$20M, worth \$240M, by issuing new shares at \$40. Assuming no synergies, what is the combined EPS?
solution: It issues $240 / 40 = 6$M shares. $EPS = 120 / 56 = 2.14$.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
In that last deal, EPS rose from \$2.00 to \$2.14 with no synergies. The combined firm's value:
- [ ] rose by 7%
- [x] didn't change: higher EPS comes with a lower P/E
- [ ] fell
solution: That's EPS bootstrapping. Value is \$2,000M + \$240M either way; the market applies a lower P/E to the combined earnings.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=open creative=true
Why do acquirers' shareholders often gain little or nothing from acquisitions, even when synergies are real?
solution: Competition among bidders and target shareholders' bargaining push the premium up toward the full value of the synergies. Managers may also overestimate synergies (overconfidence) and overpay.
:::
