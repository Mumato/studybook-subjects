title: Experiments and observational studies
---
To show that one thing **causes** another, you need to control who gets what. That's the difference between an experiment and an observational study. (OpenStax ch. 1)

::: rule
- **Observational study:** you record what already happens; you don't assign anything. It can show an **association**, not cause.
- **Experiment:** you **assign** a treatment to subjects and measure the result.
- The **explanatory variable** is what you change (the treatment); the **response variable** is what you measure.
- A **confounding variable** is linked to both and can explain the association instead.
- Good experiments use a **control group**, **random assignment** to groups, and, when possible, a **placebo** and **blinding** (subjects, and in double-blind studies also the researchers, don't know who got what).
:::

::: example
Researchers notice that people who drink more coffee have more heart problems. Smokers drink more coffee and also have more heart problems, so smoking is a **confounding variable**. To test coffee itself, randomly assign volunteers to drink coffee or decaf (a placebo) for a year, without telling them which.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=choice
Researchers look at hospital records and find that people who walk daily have lower blood pressure. This is:
- [x] an observational study
- [ ] an experiment
- [ ] a census
solution: Nobody was assigned to walk. The researchers only recorded what happened.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=choice
120 volunteers are randomly split: half get a new sleep pill, half get a sugar pill. Hours of sleep are recorded. The response variable is:
- [ ] the type of pill
- [x] hours of sleep
- [ ] the 120 volunteers
- [ ] the sugar pill
solution: The response is what's measured. The pill is the explanatory variable.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=choice
In that study, the sugar pill is a:
- [x] placebo
- [ ] confounding variable
- [ ] response variable
- [ ] stratum
solution: A fake treatment that looks the same, so expectations affect both groups equally.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=choice
Why assign subjects to groups **at random**?
- [ ] To make the groups bigger
- [x] To make the groups alike in everything except the treatment
- [ ] So the subjects don't know their group
- [ ] So that no placebo is needed
solution: Randomizing spreads other differences (age, health, habits) evenly, so a difference in results can be blamed on the treatment.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=choice
In a **double-blind** experiment:
- [ ] subjects choose their own group
- [ ] only the subjects don't know who gets the treatment
- [x] neither the subjects nor the people measuring results know who gets the treatment
- [ ] there is no control group
solution: Both sides are "blind", so nobody's expectations can bias the results.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
Towns with more fire trucks at a fire have more damage. A confounding variable is most likely:
- [x] the size of the fire
- [ ] the colour of the trucks
- [ ] the time of year
- [ ] the day of the week
solution: Big fires bring more trucks **and** cause more damage. The trucks don't cause the damage.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=choice creative=true
A study finds that students who eat breakfast get better grades. Which conclusion is justified?
- [ ] Eating breakfast causes better grades
- [ ] Better grades cause students to eat breakfast
- [x] Breakfast and grades are associated; this study alone can't show cause
solution: It's observational: other factors (sleep, family routine) could explain both. An experiment with random assignment would be needed to show cause.
:::
