title: Light as particles: quanta
---
Around 1900, light refused to behave only like a wave. Shine light on a metal and electrons pop out, but only if the light's *colour* is right, however bright it is. Einstein's answer: light comes in packets. (OpenStax College Physics 2e ch. 29)

::: rule
- Light comes in packets of energy called **photons**. Each photon's energy depends on its frequency:
$$E = h f$$
  where $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s (Planck's constant).
- **Photoelectric effect:** one photon gives its energy to one electron. Only photons with a high enough frequency can free an electron. Brighter light means **more** photons, not stronger ones.
- **Wave-particle duality:** light travels like a wave but is absorbed in particle-like lumps. Electrons, too, show wave behaviour (they make interference patterns).
:::

::: example
A photon of violet light ($f = 7.5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz) carries
$$E = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 7.5 \times 10^{14}$$
which is about $5 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
A red photon ($4 \times 10^{14}$ Hz) carries about half that. Ultraviolet photons carry even more, enough to damage skin cells: sunburn.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=choice
Very bright red light shines on a metal and frees no electrons. Dim blue light frees some. Why?
![](assets/atm-photo@3x.png)
- [ ] Blue light has more photons.
- [x] Each blue photon carries more energy; red photons each have too little, however many there are.
- [ ] Red light is absorbed by the air.
solution: One photon, one electron. If a single photon is too weak, more of them don't help.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=choice
You make the blue light brighter (same colour). The metal now gives off:
- [x] more electrons, with the same maximum energy
- [ ] the same number of electrons, each with more energy
- [ ] no electrons
solution: More photons free more electrons, but each photon still has the same energy.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=numeric answer="2"
One photon has twice the frequency of another. By what factor is its energy greater?
solution: $E = hf$: twice the frequency, twice the energy.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=numeric answer="3.3" tolerance=0.01
What is the energy of a photon of frequency $5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, in units of $10^{-19}$ J? ($h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s)
solution: $6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 5 \times 10^{14} = 33 \times 10^{-20}$, which is $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=choice
Which kind of photon carries the most energy?
- [ ] infrared
- [ ] visible green
- [x] ultraviolet
- [ ] radio
solution: Ultraviolet has the highest frequency of these, so the most energy per photon.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
You can sit in front of a bright lamp all day without sunburn, but an hour of strong sunlight burns. Why?
- [ ] The lamp is not bright enough.
- [x] Sunlight includes ultraviolet photons, each energetic enough to damage skin molecules.
- [ ] Sunlight is hotter.
solution: It's the energy per photon that matters for breaking molecules, not the total brightness.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=choice
Electrons fired through two narrow slits build up an interference pattern on a screen. This shows electrons:
- [ ] are made of light
- [x] also behave like waves
- [ ] have no mass
solution: Interference is a wave behaviour. Matter, like light, has both wave and particle sides.
:::
