title: Weight, weightlessness and tides
---
Astronauts float in the space station, yet gravity there is almost as strong as on the ground. Why? Because what we *feel* as weight is the push of whatever supports us. (OpenStax College Physics 2e ch. 6)

::: rule
- Your **weight**, as you feel it and as a scale reads it, is the **support force** pushing up on you.
- In an elevator **speeding up going up** (or slowing while going down) the scale reads more: $m(g + a)$. Accelerating downward, it reads less: $m(g - a)$.
- In **free fall** nothing supports you: you feel **weightless**, even though gravity still pulls. Orbiting astronauts are in free fall all the time.
- **Tides:** the Moon pulls the near side of Earth more than the centre, and the centre more than the far side. This *difference* stretches the oceans into two bulges, so most coasts get two high tides a day.
:::

::: example
A 60 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator.
- At rest or at constant speed: $60 \times 10 = 600$ N.
- Speeding up upward at 2 m/s²: $60 \times (10 + 2) = 720$ N.
- If the cable snapped (free fall): 0 N. They'd float above the scale.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=numeric answer="480"
The same 60 kg person is in an elevator speeding up **downward** at 2 m/s². What does the scale read, in newtons?
solution: $60 \times (10 - 2) = 480$ N.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=numeric answer="600"
The elevator moves upward at a steady 5 m/s. What does the scale read for the 60 kg person, in newtons?
solution: Constant velocity means no acceleration: $60 \times 10 = 600$ N.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=numeric answer="550"
A 50 kg person rides an elevator that speeds up upward at 1 m/s². What does the scale read, in newtons?
solution: $50 \times (10 + 1) = 550$ N.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=choice
Why do astronauts float inside the International Space Station?
- [ ] There's no gravity in space.
- [ ] Gravity there is very weak.
- [x] They and the station are both falling around Earth together, so nothing presses on them.
solution: At the station's height gravity is about 90% of what it is on the ground. Everything falls together, so nothing supports anyone.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=numeric answer="2"
About how many high tides does a typical coast get each day?
solution: There are two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the far side, and Earth turns through both each day.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
The highest tides ("spring tides") happen:
- [x] at new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up
- [ ] only in spring
- [ ] at half moon
solution: When they line up, the Sun's tide adds to the Moon's.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=choice
The Sun pulls on Earth far more strongly than the Moon does. Why does the Moon cause bigger tides?
- [ ] The Moon is heavier.
- [x] Tides depend on the *difference* in pull across Earth, and the Moon is much closer, so its pull changes more from one side to the other.
- [ ] The Sun's gravity doesn't reach the oceans.
solution: The Sun is so far away that its pull is almost the same on both sides of Earth, so it stretches the oceans less.
:::
