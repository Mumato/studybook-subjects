title: Gases and moving fluids
---
We live at the bottom of an ocean of air. Its weight presses on everything, and when air or water moves, its pressure changes in ways that lift planes. (OpenStax College Physics 2e ch. 11-13)

::: rule
- **Atmospheric pressure** at sea level is about **100 000 Pa**: the weight of the air above, about 10 tonnes on every square metre. It pushes in all directions, and fluids inside us push back equally.
- **Boyle's law:** squeeze a gas at constant temperature into half the volume and its pressure doubles.
$$P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2$$
- **Bernoulli's principle:** where a moving fluid flows **faster**, its pressure is **lower**.
:::

![](assets/mat-air@3x.png)

::: example
A balloon holds 2 L of air at 100 kPa. Squeezed to 1 L at the same temperature:
$$100 \times 2 = P \times 1$$
so $P = 200$ kPa.
Release it at high altitude, where the pressure is lower, and it expands.
:::

::: example
Air flows faster over the curved top of a wing than under it. Faster air means lower pressure above, so the higher pressure below pushes the wing up: **lift**.

![](assets/mat-wing@3x.png)
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=numeric answer="300"
A gas fills 3 L at 100 kPa. It is squeezed to 1 L at the same temperature. What is its new pressure, in kPa?
solution: $100 \times 3 = P \times 1$, so $P = 300$ kPa.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=numeric answer="6"
A weather balloon holds 3 m³ of gas at ground level. It rises to where the pressure is half as much. Ignoring temperature changes, what is its volume there, in m³?
solution: Half the pressure, twice the volume: 6 m³.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=numeric answer="50000"
Air pressure of 100 000 Pa presses on a 0.5 m² tabletop. What force does the air put on the top of the table, in newtons?
solution: $F = P \times A = 100000 \times 0.5 = 50000$ N. The air under the table pushes up just as hard, so the table doesn't notice.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=choice
When you drink through a straw, what pushes the drink up the straw?
- [ ] your suction pulls it
- [x] the atmosphere pushes down on the drink's surface, and you've lowered the pressure inside the straw
- [ ] the straw's shape
solution: Sucking lowers the pressure in the straw; the outside air pressure pushes the drink up. On the Moon, a straw wouldn't work.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=choice
Why aren't we crushed by 10 tonnes of air on each square metre?
- [ ] Air has no weight.
- [x] Fluids in our body push outward with the same pressure.
- [ ] Our skin is very strong.
solution: The pressure inside balances the pressure outside.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
Two ships sailing side by side close together tend to be pushed toward each other. Why?
- [ ] They attract by gravity.
- [x] Water flowing fast between them has lower pressure than the water on their outer sides.
- [ ] Their waves push them together.
solution: Bernoulli's principle: faster flow between the hulls means lower pressure there.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=choice
During a storm, strong wind blows over a house's roof. The roof can lift off because:
- [x] fast air above lowers the pressure above the roof, while the still air inside pushes up
- [ ] the wind pushes up under the roof
- [ ] the house gets lighter
solution: The pressure difference between inside and outside pushes the roof up.
:::
