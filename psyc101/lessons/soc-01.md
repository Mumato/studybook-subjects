title: Social thinking: attributions and attitudes
---
We constantly explain other people's behaviour, and our own, and those explanations are biased in predictable ways. Our attitudes, too, bend to fit what we do. (OpenStax Psychology 2e ch. 12)

::: rule
- **Attribution:** explaining behaviour by the person (**dispositional**: "she's rude") or the situation (**situational**: "she's having a terrible day").
- **Fundamental attribution error:** over-blaming people's character and under-weighing the situation when explaining **others'** behaviour. **Self-serving bias:** crediting ourselves for successes and blaming the situation for failures.
- **Cognitive dissonance** (Festinger): the discomfort of holding beliefs that clash with our actions. We often reduce it by changing the **belief** to fit the action.
- **Persuasion:** the **central route** uses strong arguments (lasting change); the **peripheral route** uses surface cues like attractive speakers. **Foot-in-the-door:** a small request first makes a bigger one more likely to be granted.
:::

::: example
In Festinger's classic study, students did a boring task, then were paid either **1 dollar** or **20 dollars** to tell the next person it was fun. Later, the 1-dollar group rated the task as **more** enjoyable. With 20 dollars they had a good reason for lying; with 1 dollar they didn't, so to ease the dissonance they convinced themselves it really was fun.
:::

::: problem id=p1 type=choice
A driver cuts you off and you think, "What a jerk." You don't consider that he might be rushing to the hospital. This is the:
- [x] fundamental attribution error
- [ ] self-serving bias
- [ ] foot-in-the-door technique
solution: Blaming character and ignoring possible situational causes.
:::

::: problem id=p2 type=choice
You say your A came from hard work, but your C was because the test was unfair. This is the:
- [ ] fundamental attribution error
- [x] self-serving bias
- [ ] central route to persuasion
solution: Credit for success, situational blame for failure.
:::

::: problem id=p3 type=choice
Explaining a classmate's late arrival by heavy traffic is a:
- [ ] dispositional attribution
- [x] situational attribution
- [ ] cognitive dissonance
solution: It points to the circumstances, not the person.
:::

::: problem id=p4 type=choice
In Festinger's study, which group later said the boring task was more enjoyable?
- [x] the group paid 1 dollar
- [ ] the group paid 20 dollars
- [ ] both the same
solution: With too little reason to lie, they reduced dissonance by changing their attitude.
:::

::: problem id=p5 type=text answers="cognitive dissonance;dissonance"
A smoker who knows smoking is harmful feels uncomfortable and tells herself "it's not that bad for you". What is the discomfort called?
solution: Cognitive dissonance: her action and her belief clash, so she changes the belief.
:::

::: problem id=p6 type=choice
A charity asks you to wear a small badge, and a week later asks for a donation; you're more likely to give. This is the:
- [x] foot-in-the-door technique
- [ ] peripheral route
- [ ] self-serving bias
solution: Agreeing to a small request makes you more willing to agree to a larger one.
:::

::: problem id=p7 type=choice
An advert features a famous athlete but gives no facts about the product. It relies on the:
- [ ] central route to persuasion
- [x] peripheral route to persuasion
- [ ] fundamental attribution error
solution: Surface cues instead of arguments; the effects are usually shorter-lived.
:::
